## Read-through

Priscilla is reading when Lavinia arrives.

She closes the paperback with its receipt between the pages.

“Have I interrupted something?”

“They’re taking out the window.”

“Which window?”

“In the portrait.”

Lavinia sits beside the adjoining edge of the table.

“Start again.”

Priscilla turns the book toward her.

A woman has commissioned a portrait of her lover. The lover agreed to stay for the sittings, but the portrait has become difficult to finish. First the chair was wrong. Then the dress. Now the owner wants the garden left in the picture without the window through which it was visible.

“Where is she supposed to be sitting?” Lavinia asks.

“In the room.”

“With a garden behind her.”

“Yes.”

“Perhaps she should put the window back.”

“That would require another alteration.”

Lavinia laughs.

Priscilla opens the book and finds the paragraph again. She reads the painter’s objection, then the owner’s answer. The owner has suggested that a sufficiently good painter should be able to manage it.

“Does he leave?”

“He asks for more money.”

“I like him.”

“He’s already been paid twice.”

“I like him more.”

Priscilla passes her the book. Lavinia reads the passage, then turns it over to look at the back cover.

“You didn’t mention that it was a romance.”

“I said they were lovers.”

“You’ve mostly told me about the furniture.”

“There’s quite a lot of furniture.”

“Does it ever get out of their way?”

“Occasionally.”

Lavinia looks up. Priscilla takes a drink.

“Is that why the portrait takes so long?”

“No.”

“You answered that very quickly.”

“I’ve reached the part where she finds out.”

“About the furniture?”

“The portrait was finished before any of the alterations.”

Lavinia looks at the paragraph again.

“So he’s being paid to spoil it.”

“He calls it revising.”

“I’m sure he does.”

They continue discussing the painter. Lavinia thinks he should have kept the first version and painted another. Priscilla finds the passage establishing that the owner has already refused that suggestion.

“She wants that painting changed,” Priscilla says.

“Then he should charge more.”

“He does.”

For a while, they return to the impossible garden. Lavinia suggests putting the chair outside. Priscilla points out that the owner has insisted upon the room.

Lavinia begins to answer, then turns back a page.

“Does the other woman know it was finished?”

“No.”

“And she’s still coming to sit for him?”

“She agreed to stay until he was done.”

“The owner could ask her to stay.”

“And risk having her leave that afternoon?”

Lavinia lowers the book.

“That explains it.”

Priscilla does not ask what she means.

Lavinia leaves the paperback between them. The page with the painter’s objection is still open.

“Why did you keep finding reasons to come to me?”

“Your biography was six words over the limit.”

“That was one time.”

“It was true.”

“I did not say it was false.”

Priscilla says nothing.

“You could have sent a message.”

“Yes.”

“But you came yourself.”

Priscilla remains turned toward her.

Lavinia waits. There is enough time for another answer. She looks at Priscilla’s mouth, then at her eyes.

“You know what I’m asking.”

“Yes.”

Lavinia wants something to change in her face.

She slaps Priscilla across the cheek.

“Why?”

“You should have left me alone.”

The book is still open.

Lavinia reaches toward Priscilla’s cheek. Priscilla turns away before her fingers reach it.

Lavinia withdraws her hand.

Priscilla brings her bag onto her lap. She retrieves the paperback, keeps the receipt at its page, and puts it inside.

When she closes the bag, the clasp meets the leather beside its fastening.

She takes her hand away and folds it beneath her opposite arm.

Lavinia looks down.

Priscilla gets up and steps behind her chair. Her hand comes out from beneath her arm. She uses it to close the clasp. Her fingers are still unsteady.

Lavinia turns toward someone passing.

“Could I have my bill?”

Priscilla takes the bag by its handle.

Lavinia looks up at her.

“Will you stay?”

“Until the bill comes.”
