## 1. The Saved Bench, The Eraser Crumb & The Sudden Burst (Shared Daytime — No Challenge)

In the small back room of the student exhibition, the heavy door swings shut on its hydraulic arm; the rubber strip clicks against the threshold, cutting off the corridor air.

The room has no outer windows, and the high transom along the inner wall is latched shut—two coats of cream paint sealing the brass catch into its slot so not even a draft from the stairwell can enter. Under the yellow cone of the picture lamp, the dust hangs motionless.

On the wooden bench in the middle of the room, a folded scrap of paper sits on the empty right-hand seat beside Sylvia's hip.

*Taken. Back in a minute.*

The fold is pressed flat with the side of a thumbnail—her usual neat habit of turning a preference into a rule before anyone else can sit down.

“Did I say a minute?”

Sylvia looks up from the sketchbook open across her knees and lifts the folded scrap between two fingers.

“You're always late when you come from the library,” she says mildly. “Sit down before someone else takes it.”

As soon as the space beside her is occupied, she shifts the wool hem of her unbuttoned coat three inches to the right so it covers both laps. From the paper bag beside her thigh, her left hand produces half a peeled mandarin—the white pith already picked clean from the segments, the way she has prepared them since we were twelve—and places it directly into my palm.

Caught on the rib of her left cardigan cuff, half an inch from the mandarin, clings a tiny grey curl of kneaded rubber—fresh enough that it hasn't flattened into the wool, left over from erasing something she wanted off the page before the door opened.

“Your hands are cold,” she says.

“It's windy on the steps.”

“Eat that first.”

The segment is sweet and warm from her fingers. Across the wall in front of us hangs a student painting of a harbour ferry in three shades of grey.

“He left out the pier,” I say, leaning a shoulder against hers.

“Perhaps it sank.”

“Then the passengers are remarkably calm.”

Sylvia laughs—a soft, unguarded sound that moves through her shoulder into mine. For ten seconds neither of us says anything; she peels another segment for herself, puts one more into my hand without looking, and touches the tip of her pencil to the open sketchbook on her lap.

**Private self-talk — no input:** Easy. With her, nothing has to be explained first.

Resting my chin lightly on the wool of her shoulder to see what she is drawing, I look down at the top page of the sketchbook.

On the upper sheet is a neat, harmless pencil sketch of the bench and the grey ferry frame in front of us. Beside the spine lies another folded slip she was lettering with my borrowed pen: *An Improvement / Please do not touch.*

“Another notice?” I murmur against her shoulder.

“For the corner of the frame.”

“There's rubber dust in the binding. You've been drawing something else underneath.”

“No, I haven't.”

Above the picture frames, the painted-shut transom has not moved, and the door remains sealed on its rubber strip.

Even so, a sudden, narrow current of air crosses the bench from left to right—lifting the top sheet of the sketchbook right out from under Sylvia's wrist and folding it back against the binding.

Neither hand touched the paper.

Beneath the harmless sketch of the bench lies the page she was keeping covered.

The pencil lines there were not drawn today, and they were not drawn with a steady hand. It is a close study of a woman from the collarbone to the mouth—head tilted back against a bench, collar open at the hollow of the throat, one hand resting across the waist. Whoever drew it returned to the throat and the parted lips six or seven times with soft, dark 4B lead, shading over the pulse point until the paper grain roughened and lifted, and the upper half of the face was rubbed into a dark, unrecognizable blur where the heel of a palm dragged across the graphite.

On the side of Sylvia's right thumb—resting an inch from the page—sits the exact same grey smudge of soft lead.

My chin is still resting on her shoulder. Neither of us breathes for a full second.

**Private self-talk — no input:** Too many passes of the lead over the throat. The collar from Monday's café—or Lavinia's bodice at the barre.

## 2. The Absurd Stationer Justification & The Shadow Over the Page

Sylvia does not slam the sketchbook shut.

Instead, her left hand comes up, catches my wrist where it rests against her coat, and guides my own hand two inches to the right—placing my palm and cuff directly between the overhead lamp and the open page so my shadow falls straight across the roughened throat on the paper.

“Hold still,” she says softly.

“My hand is in your light.”

“It's meant to be.”

Her thumb slides under the edge of my cuff and presses against the inside of my wrist—directly over the tendon where the pencil shaded through the paper grain, her pulse fast and warm against mine.

“The paper is nearly worn through at the neck,” I say quietly, not pulling my wrist away. “Who is that meant to be?”

Against my skin, the pad of her thumb stops moving.

“It's only a figure study from the life class,” she says.

“You don't take the life class.”

“Someone left the sheet on the bench, then.”

“It's sewn into the binding of your book.”

Sylvia looks down at the stitched spine without blinking, her voice retaining its mild, orderly decision-science calm.

“The university stationer must have bound a used sheet into the pad by mistake,” she says. “Their quality control has been slipping since September.”

“With your 4B lead still on the margin?”

“I was trying to repair the throat.”

“By rubbing the face out with your palm?”

“The proportions were distracting.”

She withdraws her thumb from my wrist, folds the top sheet neatly back over the drawing, and slides the *Please do not touch* slip between the pages like a bookmark before closing the cover.

“You could have asked me plainly,” she says, standing up from the bench.

“I did ask.”

“You asked whose neck it was. That isn't a plain question.”

**Private self-talk — no input:** Not an answer, either.

She pushes the cap onto the borrowed pen and holds it out between us. For a beat her fingers stay closed around the warm barrel until my fingers brush hers to take it.

“Your pen,” she says.

Outside at the foot of the stairs, she pulls the folded *An Improvement* slip from the edge of the closed sketchbook, folds it once more in half, and drops it into the wire bin beside the rail.

“There,” she says quietly. “Now the stationer won't be blamed for it.”

## 3. The Warm Coat Pocket & The Route Fork

At the bottom of the steps, the late-afternoon air off the harbour is cool.

Instead of stepping back, Sylvia reaches down, takes my right hand—the one still cool from the stair rail—and draws it inside the deep left pocket of her own wool coat, keeping her hand closed over mine in the dark lining.

“Come with me tonight,” she says.

“Where?”

“There's an old cross-section house model in the north room on the hill. Someone left the interior bulbs on a timer, and nobody ever goes up there at six.”

“Lavinia is waiting outside the library at six. We're going for a walk.”

Inside the warm coat pocket, Sylvia's thumb passes once across the back of my knuckles.

“You were with her last night,” she says—not accusing, her voice low and steady against the wind. “And you've been rehearsing what you're going to say to her since you sat down on that bench.”

“I haven't.”

“Your hand hasn't relaxed once until now.” Her fingers close gently around mine inside the wool pocket. “Come up the hill with me. You won't have to arrange a single sentence all evening. There's still time to tell her.”

## 4. Evening Fork A (Default): Priscilla's Charm, Lavinia's Training & The Thrown Bouquet (`4A–4E`)

“Another evening?”

Inside the coat pocket, Sylvia's fingers stay over mine for one more second, then open and let my hand slip back into the cool air.

“Perhaps,” she says, and turns up the hill path with her sketchbook under her arm; the library quad lies the other way.

By six o'clock, the florist paper around the white flowers has grown damp beneath the thumb.

Lavinia's fingers ease one creased petal out of the paper fold.

“You're bruising them.”

“I was holding them out of the wind for you.”

“By strangling the stems?”

“They survived the walk up the hill.”

The wrapping slides through my fingers, though one small white blossom catches on a nail. Lavinia takes that one too, tucks the stem back into the center of the bunch, and looks up with the corner of her mouth lifting.

“You're welcome.”

“Left.”

“Which way now?” she asks from behind the flowers.

“Say it aloud. I can't see through your bribe.”

“Right. Mind the curb.”

“I know where the curb is.”

“You walked into it in February.”

“Because you were talking.” She lowers the paper an inch, laughing.

“A remarkably short walk, Priscilla.”

“The first lap is to make sure you're paying attention. I know a longer way.”

“I'm sure you do.”

She shifts the flowers into the crook of her left arm and slips her right hand into mine—fingers warm, thumb settling over the back of my knuckle as if rewarding the first lap.
