## 1. A Photograph

Sylvia slides the fuller teacup onto my mat and keeps the one with the small chip on the rim for herself.

“The tables by the door are in a draught,” she says. “You always complain about your throat when you sit under the vent.”

“And the window light happens to be behind your chair.”

“Someone had to face the glare.”

She holds her phone out at arm's length, then lowers it half an inch without taking the shot.

“Wait. Your left collar is turned under,” she says. “Don't move.”

Her free hand reaches across the corner of the table. Two cool fingers slip beneath the lapel of my blouse, smooth the linen flat against the collarbone, and withdraw before the cloth can warm them.

The chair legs scrape half an inch closer until both faces fit inside the screen.

“Not that expression,” she says.

“It's my expression.”

“It's the one you save for university circulars.”

A slight widening of the mouth.

“Worse.”

On the table between the saucers, my own phone sounds.

**Lavinia, message:** Try not to look too pleased when you see me tomorrow.

Eleven words. No greeting, and *tomorrow* at the end.

Above the keyboard bar, a small grey line appears: *Lavinia is recording an audio message...*

The three dots pulse between the cups for a few seconds, then vanish without sending, leaving the bar blank.

Sylvia takes the photograph.

“Oh,” she says, lowering the phone. “You were looking down. Shall I delete that one for you?”

“Let me see it first.”

She brings her phone between us. In the picture, the gaze is dropped slightly below the lens—still toward the table where the message lit—while Sylvia has caught herself watching the side of my face.

“Who was it?”

“Lavinia.”

“I should have asked her to help earlier.”

Two fingers spread across the glass to enlarge the picture. Sylvia leans against my arm to see what is being inspected.

“Don't inspect it that closely.”

“I like it.”

“That isn't usually why you zoom in.”

**Self-talk:** Her face in this one, too.

### The Photograph Passage Rejoins

Sylvia puts her phone away and moves her chair to the other side of the table.

The paperback beneath Sylvia's bag has a bent cover. She smooths it with her palm.

*The Funeral Rehearsal.*

“She insists on attending,” Sylvia says.

“Her own?”

“Listen.”

She opens it near the beginning.

“‘The eulogy was excellent. She asked whether it could be about her.’”

A laugh escapes before the cup reaches the saucer. Sylvia's finger stays beneath the sentence.

“I read that standing in the shop.”

“You bought a whole novel for one line?”

“I wanted to know what she'd object to next.”

The corner of the next page lifts under her thumb.

## 2. A Reading

Sylvia lowers her voice.

“‘Again,’ said the aunt. ‘You sound relieved.’”

“Is she in the coffin?”

“An armchair.”

“More convenient for supervising.”

Sylvia finds the next speech.

“There's a man at the door. He says he's come about a carriage.”

Sylvia puts one hand against her chest.

“‘My cousin required the carriage.’”

A laugh catches before she has finished the last word.

“What?”

“The voice.”

“He's in love.”

“Give me his part.”

She turns the book toward me, keeping a finger beneath the line. I read his next speech in the same voice.

Sylvia bends over the table. A moment passes before she can stop laughing.

“Too pleased with himself,” she says. “He thinks this is working.”

The line repeats without the flourish on the last word.

“Yes. Like that.”

She takes the book back.

For a while, we read in turns. Sylvia gives the aunt the careful, carrying voice of someone repeating a reasonable request. When I try to do her as well, Sylvia kicks the leg of my chair.

“Mine.”

“All right.”

**Self-talk:** The aunt again. Before he can finish.

The phone lights up on the table.

**Lavinia, message:** Are you busy?

**Priscilla, message:** Reading with Sylvia.

**Lavinia, message:** Anything good?

**Priscilla, message:** She's making it better.

“Should I send it to you?” Sylvia asks.

“What?”

“My part. You're answering those quite quickly.”

The message goes through; the phone returns to the wood.

“Sorry. Where are we?”

She puts her finger beneath the line.

One exchange, then another, before a hand stops her at the margin.

“He says the flowers are for the funeral.”

“They are now.”

“They've got the niece's name on them.”

“The aunt has taken the ribbon off.”

“He could say he wanted to see her.”

Sylvia offers the book across the saucers.

“Then say it.”

“I wanted to see you.”

She looks down at the page; her finger stays still for half a beat.

“Much shorter.”

“They could have saved paper.”

Sylvia begins the woman's answer without the voice. Halfway through, she remembers it, and the place on the page is lost in laughing.

The aunt invites everyone back for another rehearsal.

“‘Tomorrow, do try to look a little less pleased,’” Sylvia reads.

“Let me see.”

Sylvia turns the book toward me.

*Tomorrow.* At the beginning, this time.

“Have you read this part before?”

“No. Why?”

“Keep going.”

She waits. The corner of the page is still between my fingers.

The fingers open; she draws the book back.

The phone lights again.

Sylvia closes the paperback around her finger.

“What does she want?”

“She's asking whether she's interrupting.”

“And?”

Only a few pages between her finger and the front cover.

“We haven't got very far.”

“No.”

Sylvia puts the book down.

“Let me answer her,” she says. “Then you don't have to stop.”

“What are you going to say?”

“I'll show you before I send it.”

The unlocked phone passes into her hand with the conversation open.

She types, then turns the screen toward me.

**Unsent draft on Priscilla's phone:** You're interrupting my date. Sylvia.

“Your date?”

“You've stayed. We've taken a photograph. I've read to you.”

“Would you like me to leave?”

“Not yet.”

Her thumb rests below the message. She has not sent it.

**Self-talk:** Changing *date* to *reading* would still be a joke. It would be easier to explain.

“Send it.”

Sylvia sends the message and gives the phone back.

It rests beside the drink. Neither hand reaches for the book.

**Lavinia, message:** Priscilla?

Sylvia looks across the table.

“You should answer that one.”

Two thumbs on the glass:

**Priscilla, message:** I said she could send it.

Sent before anything else can follow it.

Sylvia opens the book and reads the first few words. The cue passes without a sound.

**Lavinia, message:** Then tell me yourself.

Across the table, Sylvia is still holding the page open.

## 3. Five Alternative Continuations (`A–E`)

### A — Never started: an ordinary provocation

The message is recorded once.

**Priscilla, sent voice:** I'm having a very good time. I don't think I want to leave yet.

Sylvia watches it go.

“Was that my review?”

“You can put it on the cover.”

She turns the book over and considers the printed endorsements.

The reply arrives.

**Lavinia, message:** Then I'll leave you to it.

Sylvia finds the page again.

The phone stays in the hand a little longer.

**Self-talk:** No question at the end.

“Your part,” Sylvia says.

The first line starts at the wrong row.

“No. Above that.”

Before the speech is finished, the eyes drop to the phone again.

Sylvia takes her finger out of the book.

“Do you want to call her?”

“Why would I?”

“I don't know. You tell me.”

The phone goes face down on the wood.

“One more page?”

Sylvia pushes the book across the table.

“You read.”

The first sentence comes out too loud; she makes a small downward motion with her hand, and the voice drops back into place.

At the last line she supplies the aunt's voice, almost under her breath.

A laugh; Sylvia smiles, then closes the book.

“That will do.”

She puts it in her bag. Outside the café, we walk together as far as the stairs. Sylvia turns there; the corridor ahead continues with the phone in my hand.

### B — Started, unfinished: an answer postponed

The voice recorder opens, then closes without recording.

“Not now.”

Thumbs type instead:

**Priscilla, message:** I'll answer you later.

The phone goes face down on the table.

Sylvia watches until the hand leaves it.

“Finished?”

“For now.”

She turns the paperback around.

“You can be the cousin.”

“Does she have a line, or only a grievance?”

“A whole speech.”

The cousin has brought the wrong eulogy. Sylvia points to its first sentence.

“‘Beloved father of six,’” I read.

Sylvia shuts her eyes.

A laugh breaks the next word.

“No,” she says. “There's an answer.”

Before the sentence can finish, Sylvia supplies the aunt's answer:

“‘Three,’ she said. ‘And I was their mother.’”

We read to the end of the chapter. Once, the phone is turned over—no new message—and set down again.

When Sylvia begins looking for the next chapter, a fingertip touches the edge of the page.

“Shall we stop there?”

She checks the time.

“We probably should.”

She folds the corner back along its old crease and puts the book in her bag. At the stairs she reminds me that the cousin has my voice now; doing the voice once more sends her down laughing.

### C — Won, not Perfect: a direct answer

The phone moves closer.

**Priscilla, recording:** I'm enjoying my afternoon. I'm also looking forward to seeing you tomorrow.

The recording plays back once; the small breath between the sentences stays in place, and then it goes through.

Sylvia waits.

“Finished?”

“Yes.”

The book returns to the middle of the table.

The reply comes before the place is found.

**Lavinia, message:** Tomorrow, then. Finish your date.

The phone goes face down.

“What did she say?” Sylvia asks.

“To finish.”

“Very sensible.”

Sylvia gives back the man's part. The man has been asked to read the eulogy.

“With the flowers still in her lap?”

“Apparently.” Sylvia turns the page. “Wait. I want to hear this.”

She reaches across to point out a sentence and knocks the spoon onto the saucer; my hand moves the cup so that she can leave her wrist on the page.

**Self-talk:** I missed this line before.

“‘Her absence,’ I read, ‘will leave a silence—’”

“‘Louder,’ said the aunt.”

We continue until Sylvia puts the book down of her own accord.

“Enough?”

“One more.”

“One.”

She reads the woman's answer; my eyes stay on her until she has finished. At the stairs, Sylvia puts the paperback in her bag and says the man's voice is ruined for her now.

“You can do him differently.”

“I don't want to now.”

She goes down before an answer can follow.

### D — Lost: the rehearsal is sent

“She wants a voice message.”

“Then use the man's voice,” Sylvia says, her finger still inside the paperback. “Tell her your cousin required the carriage.”

“She'd hang up before the verb.”

“Not if you sound sufficiently tragic.”

The thumb presses the microphone icon on the screen.

“I'm having a very good time,” I say into the phone—in the suitor's appalling, half-dying voice.

Across the saucers, Sylvia loses her composure completely. She bends over the book, one hand against her mouth.

“Stop,” she says through the laugh. “You sound as though you're in the coffin. Give it here—‘I'm having a very good time, and I don't think I want to leave yet.’”

Her voice starts in prim demonstration and breaks into a warm, helpless laugh on the last word.

The thumb lifts from the glass. The recording sits in the preview bar between our cups.

Sylvia catches her breath and looks at the lit bar on the screen.

“You were actually recording that?”

“It hasn't gone through.”

“Erase it,” she says, still half-smiling as she reaches for her tea. “We sound ridiculous.”

The preview runs once between the saucers: my absurd first line, the catch of her breath, and then her voice leaning close across the table—*I'm having a very good time, and I don't think I want to leave yet.*

**Self-talk:** She sounds as though she means it.

The thumb taps *Send*.

Sylvia's hand stops beside the book.

“You sent it.”

“You sounded pleased.”

“That wasn't for her. I told you to erase it.”

“I heard you.”

Sylvia takes the paperback back to her side of the table. For a moment she holds it closed in both hands.

“Then why?”

“It sounded better than mine would.”

She puts it in her bag.

“Give me a moment,” I say.

“You've had one.”

She stands. Her chair catches against the table leg; she pulls it free without looking down, while my hand moves the cup out of her way.

The phone lights on the table.

**Lavinia, message:** Call me when you're alone.

Sylvia looks once at the table, takes her own phone, and says, “Enjoy your call.”

Through the doorway, she has already passed out of sight.

**Self-talk:** I can do that now.

Still, the chair stays where it is long enough for the screen to go dark.

## 5. Shared Coda: After

A zip runs close to Lavinia's microphone.

“Hold on.”

Something scrapes. She breathes out sharply.

“Too much?” I ask.

“For the case.”

“Take something out.”

“I want it.”

A corner of the mouth lifts in the quiet room.

She tries the zip again.

“What are you taking?”

“I haven't decided what I'm leaving.”

“That's going well.”

“Very.”

For a while the line carries only the sound of her moving things. Once she laughs at something unseen.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That was a laugh.”

“Yes.”

Silence waits for her to continue.

“You'll see tomorrow,” she says.

After a moment, the zip closes.

“There.”

The chair turns back toward the desk.

“Stay on the call.”

“I've finished.”

“I know.”

A faint knock comes through the phone.

“Priscilla.”

“Yes?”

“You don't have to stay.”

“Would you like me to go?”

“I asked if you wanted to.”

“No.”

“All right.”

The phone comes a little closer to the chin; something brushes against the microphone.

“Put it down,” Lavinia says. “I can hear you moving it.”

It settles flat beside the lamp.

“Like that?”

“Yes.”

The desk is lit in a small circle; the rest of the room is dark.

“Don't hang up when I stop talking,” Lavinia says.

“I wasn't going to.”

She asks what was so funny about the book.

“She keeps stopping her own funeral.”

“Is she dead?”

“She's practising.”

Into the quiet room, in the aunt's voice: “‘Again. You sound relieved.’”

The laugh comes through the phone, then a rustle.

When she becomes quiet, the phone stays where it is.
