## 1. Beat 0 & Beat 1 — The Sheltered Chair, The High Louver Shadows & `"will"`

Rain strikes the high louver glass near the ceiling of Room 2.17 in uneven gusts, turning the afternoon light the color of wet slate.

The room is six paces from wall to wall—no eye-level window, only the slanted ceiling louvers streaming with water above and the tall frosted glass pane of the corridor door. Above the doorframe, the metal louver of the ceiling vent hums softly, sending a cool draft across the terrazzo floor toward the door side of the table.

There are two chairs in the room: a curved-back wooden chair standing in the sheltered corner under the warm amber wall lamp, out of the vent draft, and a narrower flat-slat chair on the cold side of the table near the door.

I leave the curved-back chair in the sheltered corner empty and sit on the cold side of the table.

A moment later, the door swings inward. Lavinia steps across the threshold, shaking rain from the collar of her coat.

“You were waiting,” she says.

“I came in early.”

Before the door latch has settled behind her back, a tall shadow slows outside in the corridor. Through the frosted glass panel, the blurred silhouette of a flat cardboard portfolio rests under a left arm.

No knock comes.

Instead, a left hand rises outside the pane, slips the slotted loop of a white card over the outer brass handle, and smooths the paper flat against the glass before footsteps recede quietly down the hall.

Through the frosted glass, the black lettering floats in reverse at the height of Lavinia's shoulder:

*Quiet assessment in progress. Please do not disturb.*

Lavinia glances once at the reversed letters on the glass, then looks down at the curved-back wooden chair standing in the warm corner under the wall lamp—and at the plain chair I took in the draft by the door.

Before she sits, two fingertips of her right hand rest for half a second on the top rail of the curved chair back.

Then both of her hands take the chair by its back, lift it clear of the floor, and set it down ninety degrees from the table—facing my chair directly.

When she sits, less than two inches remain between our knees.

“Is the chair comfortable?” I ask.

“It was facing the table.”

“You changed that.”

“Yes.”

From her chosen angle, the programme folder on the desk cannot be read without turning her head away from my face.

The chair stays where she set it; only the folder moves—lifted across the corner of the table into her direct line of sight.

“You move everything,” Lavinia says.

“Only the folder.”

Outside in the corridor, two sets of footsteps approach the door. Their tall, headless shadows stretch through the frosted glass across the ceiling and slide down the wall behind Lavinia's throat—slowing beside the reversed white card on the handle, dropping to a murmur, and passing on.

Inside the six-pace room, Lavinia picks up the red pencil. For a while the only sound under the rain is the turn of paper as we check the staging sequence—where my draft shortened the transition walk from the wings so she does not cross the full diagonal before the variation.

Lavinia's eyes linger on that shortened stage-cross note for a beat. She does not object to it. Instead, the tip of her red pencil stops two lines lower, beneath the sentence describing the performance for visitors.

“You wrote *will*,” she says, striking a single red line through the word.

“You crossed it out.”

“I noticed.”

“There are two more,” I say, touching a fingertip to the lower margin instead of taking the pencil from her hand.

Lavinia looks from my finger to my face, then draws two clean red lines through the remaining modal verbs. By the bottom of the sheet, the page holds a third version that neither of us would have written alone.

## 2. Beat 2 — The Yellow Slip Under the Pen, The Refused Portrait & The Cheekbone Pressure

As Lavinia sets the red pencil down, the cuff of her cardigan brushes a black pen lying at the upper edge of the desk blotter.

Pinned beneath the barrel is a small square of yellow paper, folded once on a slight left-handed diagonal and pressed flat with the side of a thumbnail.

When Lavinia's sleeve shifts the pen, the yellow fold springs half-open against the wood.

Inside, in Sylvia's neat, upright hand, are two lines:

*I moved the curved chair out of the vent draft. That corner stays warmer.*

My hand stops on the edge of the folder.

Lavinia's fingers rest on the barrel of the black pen. She reads the two lines once, looks down at the curved wooden arm under her elbow, and then looks across at my chair in the cold draft by the door.

“You didn't move this chair,” she says quietly.

“It was there when I came in.”

“And you took the other one.”

Without lifting her eyes from mine, Lavinia's thumb nudges the black pen across the polished wood—pushing the folded yellow slip ahead of the barrel, half an inch, another inch—until both the pen and the yellow note slide over the far edge of the desk and drop into the wastepaper basket beside the table leg.

“The red pencil works better,” she says evenly.

Before my hand can move toward the basket, her other hand draws her phone from her coat pocket.

The camera shutter clicks once in the six-pace room.

“Don't.”

“You took mine on Monday's screen.”

“That was Sylvia's phone.”

“And this is mine.”

The small rectangle of light tilts toward her chin—reflected twice in the dark, rain-streaked louver panes near the ceiling.

“Delete it,” I say.

Lavinia looks down at the image on her glass, then up at me.

“May I keep it? Just for myself.”

“You knew I didn't want one taken.”

“I know.”

I lean forward across the two-inch gap between our chairs to see the screen.

In the photograph, my hand is still half-raised above the empty corner of the blotter where the yellow slip lay a second ago, my mouth unarranged.

At the corner of my mouth, a smile begins—and stops halfway as the muscle at the right cheekbone pulls slightly.

Lavinia's gaze leaves the lit screen and fixes on my right cheek.

“Does it still hurt?” she asks quietly.

Rain thickens against the high louver window. On the outer handle, the reversed white card keeps the hallway empty.

“Are we finished for today?” Lavinia asks, her eyes dropping to the second folder beside her sleeve.

### 5B — Started, unfinished: the deleted frame & the untouched folder

Fingertips touch the cardboard cover of the second folder, then draw back.

“There is another folder, but it can wait,” I say. “Delete the picture first.”

Lavinia looks at my hand leaving the folder, then down at the phone in her lap.

“You're certain?”

“Yes.”

Two taps on the glass; she turns the empty camera roll toward the desk, stands, and leaves the wooden chair still facing mine. At the door, she takes the reversed white card off the outer handle and drops it onto my desk blotter.

### 5D — Lost: the locked-knees bargain & the wet stocking wiped up Priscilla's shin (With Self-Talk Selector & Default)

The second folder slides across the edge of the desk until its first page overlaps her knee.

“The visitor notes are still inconsistent,” I say.

“They are not due today.”

“No.”

“And if I don't feel like checking them?”

Four fingers of my left hand rest across the top edge of her phone—not taking it from her palm, only tilting the lit screen down against the dark wool of her skirt.

“Then delete the photograph before you open the door,” I say softly. “Or stay until the second folder is finished, and you may keep it.”

In the slate-grey light of the six-pace room, Lavinia's eyes narrow.

“You're bargaining with me.”

“You asked whether you could keep it.”

Three seconds of silence. Outside in the corridor, a distant bell rings and fades behind the notice on the glass.

Lavinia clicks the phone screen dark and drops it into her coat pocket.

Then she hooks the toe of her right shoe behind the front wooden leg of my chair and pulls it sharply forward—sliding my chair two inches closer until our knees lock together beneath the desk edge and my right thigh is caught squarely between hers.

The force of dragging my chair slips the rain-wet leather heel of her loafer off her foot.

Under the table, without looking down, she lifts her stockinged foot and presses the rain-soaked arch against the inside of my ankle—sliding the wet nylon slowly up my shin, wiping the cold rainwater from her heel onto my skin and hem until the clammy chill soaks through to the bone and her own foot warms against my calf.

My leg shivers once; her knees close firmer around my thigh to hold my shin still against her wet sole.

She picks up the red pencil and sets her free hand flat over my knee.

“Your ankle was dry,” she says quietly.

“Yours is wet.”

“Not anymore. Open page one.”
