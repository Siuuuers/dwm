## 1. Beat 0 — The Completed Knock, The Left-Turned Blue Mug & `"I Like It Stronger Now"`

At the top of the stairs, my right hand rises to knock on the flat door; my left hand holds the closed long black umbrella.

Just before my knuckles reach the wood, the door swings inward.

Lavinia stands on the threshold in her stockinged feet. Without a word, her hand takes the closed long umbrella from my left hand and sets it inside the hallway stand.

My raised right hand stays arrested in the air for half a beat—then redirects two inches to the wooden doorjamb and completes one light, exact knock against the frame.

Lavinia watches the knuckles finish the knock against the wood before she steps back to let me cross the threshold.

In the narrow kitchen off the hall, the kettle switch clicks down on the first press.

No need to look for the light switch; the pale strip under the upper cabinet is already on.

Right hand to the second cupboard from the window: two thin porcelain cups on the lower shelf, the one with the hairline chip turned toward the wall so only my own fingers will take it. Left hand to the tin behind the flour jar: loose black tea, then the small square honey pot.

Beside the sink, on the wire drying rack, sits a third mug—heavy blue stoneware, not part of the flat's set. Three clear drops of water still cling to the inner rim, and the handle is turned to the left.

On the threshold behind the counter, Lavinia's stockinged feet come to a stop.

In the dark glass of the kitchen window above the sink, her reflection leans one shoulder against the doorframe, arms folded over her cardigan, watching my hands at the counter.

“You still remember where the honey is,” she says.

“It hasn't moved.”

“The cups moved.”

“Only to the left side of the shelf.”

My fingers lift the heavy blue stoneware mug from the wire rack, dry its base once with the dish towel, and place it on the highest shelf above the refrigerator—behind the glass salad bowl, out of sight from the doorway.

In the window glass above the sink, the corner of Lavinia's mouth curves.

“She brought soup on Wednesday,” Lavinia says calmly. “After the exhibition.”

“How thoughtful of her,” I say, opening the tea tin. “She always likes to leave a container she has to come back for.”

Lavinia steps out of the doorway and comes up beside my elbow at the counter, close enough that her sleeve brushes mine as I measure one spoonful of loose leaves into the warmed pot.

“A little more,” she says.

I pause with the spoon above the tin. “You used to say a second spoonful made it bitter.”

“I tried it your way,” she says, looking down into the dark leaves. “I like it stronger now.”

Half a spoonful more drops into the pot.

“Like that?”

“Yes.”

Boiling water fills the porcelain pot, and we carry the tray into the living room.

## 2. Beat 1 — Priscilla's Wrist Over Her Eyes, The Impatient `"Nine"` & The Three Silent Turns

As I set the tray on the low table, my hand goes to the wall switch beside the bookcase and clicks off the overhead bulb—leaving only the low amber shade in the corner and the pale blue square of the television across the rug.

Lavinia does not look up from the sofa cushion as the ceiling light goes out.

“The glare?” she asks.

“It reflects in the screen.”

“It never bothered you before.”

From the corner of the sofa, Lavinia looks toward the television.

“They've been in that corridor for ten minutes,” she says.

“Nine,” I say.

“That isn't better.”

“No. He's opened the same door four times without once checking the latch first.” My fingers tap once against the rim of the saucer. “If he wanted to be inside the room, he'd have taken the key by now.”

On the screen, the man in the wet raincoat opens a heavy mahogany door, steps through the frame, and finds himself walking down the exact same carpeted corridor from the opposite end.

“We could stop it,” she says.

“I'd like to see whether he ever reaches a room.”

Lavinia settles back against the cushion. The pale blue light from the television moves across the hollow of her throat and the corner of her mouth.

Without turning her head toward my chair, she asks: “What did he just take out of his pocket?”

Silence for a beat.

“A key?” I say.

“He didn't put his hand in his pocket.” Lavinia turns her head on the cushion and catches my eyes on the side of her face instead of the television. “Watch the film, not me.”

Beside her saucer on the low table, her phone plays a slow piano recording at low volume. With her teacup resting against her knee, she hums a short four-note phrase—coming in half a beat before the piano reaches it, while our voices cover the middle bars of the track.

“You're early,” I say.

“Not on the other recording.”

When the track changes, I look toward her phone beside the teapot. “What was the one before this? We talked through half of it.”

“You were answering me.”

“I won't this time.”

Lavinia restarts the piano track—and before she settles back, I set my cup down on the saucer and tip my head toward the narrow strip of rug between the low table and the television.

“Shall we?”

Lavinia looks at my offered hand, then at the phone.

“Not to this. Wait.”

She picks up her phone from beside the teapot, scrolls through two lines, and starts the other recording. After listening to its opening bars, she sets the phone down on the wood.

“This one.”

I stand and offer my hand.

Lavinia takes it. When we step into the narrow space beside the table, she lays her left hand on my shoulder; I step closer and rest my right palm at the small of her back.

As we begin, my gaze drops once toward the edge of the rug.

Lavinia immediately makes her next step smaller.

“You're still watching your feet,” she says softly.

“This is an improvement.”

She laughs.

We do not move far. Lavinia begins a slow turn and feels me follow; on the next bar, I change direction first, and she adjusts her weight without saying anything. The low table passes out of her line of sight, then returns.

When the song finishes, I glance toward the phone on the table. “Another?”

“Not yet.”

Lavinia keeps her hand in mine. Without any music in the room—only the faint hum of the television and the man on screen opening another door onto the same hallway—she takes one more slow step across the rug, and I move with her for three silent turns.

Finally she stops.

“I want my tea,” she murmurs.

My hand opens from hers, and we return to our seats.

## 3. Beat 2 — `"I Want to Hear You Say It Before You See It"`, The Taped Note & The Crossed-Out Script

Lavinia picks up her cup and takes a sip of the stronger tea.

“In the story we read before I went,” she says quietly over the rim, “you said the woman at the end was right to send her away.”

“I changed my mind the next day,” I say.

“You wrote it down.”

Her hand reaches to the low shelf beside the sofa and lifts a dark-green linen notebook—but instead of opening it, she holds it closed against her chest, her fingers pressing into the linen cover.

“May I see it?” I ask.

Lavinia does not open the cover. In the blue light from the television, her eyes stay on mine—quieter now, without the teasing edge.

“Say it first,” she whispers.

“You already have the note in your hand.”

“I know what the paper says. I've had the paper for months.” Her fingers tighten slightly on the green cover. “I want to hear you say it before you see it.”

On the screen, the corridor door swings shut in silence.

“You were right about the ending,” I say softly. “She shouldn't have let her go.”

For a second Lavinia's throat moves as she swallows. Then she opens the green cover to a marked page and passes it across the low table into my hands.

Fixed to the upper margin of the page with a strip of yellowed tape is a small square of paper in my handwriting:

*You were right about the ending.*

“Just that page,” she murmurs.

“I still disliked the last chapter.”

“You didn't put that in the note.”

When the next track on her phone ends in a soft click, Lavinia turns half-sideways on the sofa to look through her recordings.

The green notebook lies open across my knees.

Beneath the bottom edge of my taped note, the lower half of the page is covered in Lavinia's slanted dark ink—two lines in Romanian at the top, and below them an exchange in English laid out like a play script.

In the second line, she had written the milder word *leave* first, struck it through twice with a heavy nib, and rewritten it harder above the line:

> *P: I'd do it again if you limped.*  
> *L: Even if I told you to ~~leave~~ get out?*  
> *P: Don't tell me to get out when you've already taken my coat.*

And at the very bottom margin of the page, underlined twice in the same dark ink:

*Don't ask her to stay.*

## 4. Beat 3 — The Forced Rehearsal ("Auditioning the Line") & The Five Challenge Continuations (`6A–6E`)

Lavinia looks back from her phone before starting the track.

Her eyes drop immediately to my thumb holding the lower corner of the taped note up from the page.

“Not that page,” she says, extending her hand palm-up between us.

The notebook stays open across my knees.

“You wrote my part,” I say quietly.

“Give it back.”

“Do I sound like that to you?”

Slowly, Lavinia lowers her extended hand to her knee. In the blue flicker from the television—where the man in the raincoat is opening another identical door—her eyes sharpen.

“A minute ago you had to say it before you saw it,” she says, her English suddenly crisp and stripped of play. “Now that you've seen this one without asking, let's hear you say it aloud.”

“Lavinia—”

“Go on. The first line. Let's hear whether I got your voice right.”

My eyes drop to the slanted ink on the page.

“*I would be concerned if the pain returned.*”

“Stop.” Lavinia leans forward on the sofa, her palm striking the wool cushion beside her hip. “That isn't what is written on the page. Don't polish it. You usually only amend my documents when I'm in another room.”

“Read what is written, Priscilla,” she says softly, her eyes gleaming in the blue light. “From the top. Without improving me.”

### 6D — Lost: tearing out the bottom instruction & the cuff pull (With Self-Talk Selector & Default)

The torn strip of paper folds once and slips into my pocket. The notebook goes onto the low table.

“Amending the ending,” I say softly. “You left a contradiction at the bottom.”

“Tell me without holding my sleeve.”

“You're insufferable,” she whispers against my mouth, her free hand tangling into the hair behind my ear and drawing my head down until our foreheads strike lightly together. “You think everything in this flat belongs to you.”
