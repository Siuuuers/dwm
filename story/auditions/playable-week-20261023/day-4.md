## 0. The Ajar Door, The Independent Laugh & The Two Shortened Stems

In the rehearsal room next door, a practice piano plays a slow four-bar phrase, pauses to correct a chord, and begins the same four notes again.

Outside Studio B, the heavy door is ajar by less than an inch. Over the piano next door, two voices are talking near the wooden bench—unhurried, mid-conversation.

“...you've made the chin too square.”

“Then stop moving your head when you talk.”

“I wasn't talking. I was breathing.”

“Do less of that for thirty seconds.”

A short, genuine laugh from the floorboards—Lavinia's—followed by the dry rustle of heavy paper closing.

“I'll bring the rest on Friday,” the other voice says quietly.

When the main door swings inward under the hand a moment later, the far side door by the dressing passage is just settling into its frame with a soft click.

On the narrow wooden ledge beside the bench stands a low glass jar of water. Inside it sit yesterday's white flowers—two stems cut three inches shorter than the rest so their bruised heads float just above the rim.

## 1. Beat 1 — The Warm-Water Test, The Thursday Excuse & The Underlined Page

Lavinia sits on the rosin-warmed floorboards with her back resting against the front of the low wooden bench, both legs stretched out loosely in front of her.

When the space on the bench behind her is taken, she leans the back of her head directly against my knee and reaches up for the paper cup.

Her fingers close around the warm cardboard.

“That isn't iced.”

“The ice machine in the canteen was being cleaned.”

“In October.”

“They clean it on Thursdays.”

“You're a terrible liar when you don't need to be.”

“You're drinking it.”

“Because my hands are cold.” She takes a slow mouthful over the rim, her head still resting against the wool of my skirt, and glances sideways at the open canvas bag beside my shoe. A paperback protrudes from the top, a strip of paper marking the middle pages.

Without asking, her free hand reaches into the bag, draws the book out, and opens it to the marker.

“Page eighty,” she says, tilting the leaf toward the window light. “Read the part you underlined twice.”

“It's for tomorrow's seminar.”

“You underlined it twice because you were pleased with your own point. Start from the top.” She settles the back of her neck more comfortably against my knee and watches my reflection in the wall-length mirror across the floorboards.

Looking down at the page over her shoulder, I begin reading.

By the fourth sentence, the footnote at the bottom of the page is clearly wrong about the heroine's motive, and it takes three more sentences to explain why the author ruined the chapter by making her apologize too soon.

In the mirror across the room, Lavinia is no longer looking at the paperback; she is watching my mouth.

When I pause at the margin note, she reaches up without turning her head and takes my free hand, drawing my palm down until it rests against her collarbone above the wool cardigan.

“You still drop the end of the sentence,” she murmurs.

“You still lean back to hear it.”

“Before I went, I leaned in.”

“And now?”

Her thumb passes once across the knuckle of my index finger.

“Keep reading,” she says softly.

## 2. Beat 2 — The Slow-Burn Deduction: From Sill Dust to Sylvia's 4B Graphite Stick

“It's warm in here,” Lavinia says, releasing my hand only to slip her wool cardigan off her shoulders.

As she shifts sideways to lay the cardigan on the bench, the late-afternoon light catches a dark shadow along the pale rib-seam of her practice bodice just above the left hip.

At first glance it looks like a streak of grey dust from the window sill or the underside of the bench.

Then she leans half an inch toward the mirror, and the shadow separates into four distinct marks.

A wider oval pressed into the wool in front; three narrower lines curving back toward the spine.

A left hand.

And set two inches higher on the ribcage than any dance partner places his hand for a lift—not at the hip bone where weight is taken, but high on the ribs, where someone standing directly in front of the bench would take a sitter's waist in her left hand to turn the shoulder toward the window light.

Before Lavinia can lean her head back against my knee, a folded linen handkerchief is already between my fingers, dampened against the warm condensation on the cup lid.

“Hold still,” I say.

“What are you doing?”

The damp linen presses against the front mark, expecting dry dust to lift off the wool.

It does not lift. Under the wet cloth, the grey mark smears darker—velvety, heavy, and slightly greasy against the pale weave.

Only one person in this building uses soft, unlacquered 4B woodless graphite sticks that coat the heel of the left palm and the inside of the fingers because she dislikes the noise of a pencil sharpener in quiet rooms. Yesterday afternoon in the exhibition room, that exact grey film sat along the side of her thumb when she handed me half a mandarin; twenty minutes ago on these floorboards, she didn't even wipe her left hand on her rag before putting her fingers around Lavinia's waist.

**Private self-talk — no input:** She held her ribs with the lead still on her skin.

Beneath the thin bodice, the muscle over Lavinia's ribs flinches once, then goes taut under the sudden force of my hand.

“There's pencil lead on your waist,” I say, my voice dropping half a tone.

“She was turning my shoulder toward the window.”

“With her bare hand on your ribs?”

“I kept moving when I breathed.” In the mirror across the floorboards, Lavinia watches the muscle in my jaw tighten—and the corner of her mouth lifts in a slow, deliberate provocation. “Her hands were warm. Unlike yours.”

The second pass of the damp linen scrubs hard enough to pull the seam of the bodice sideways against her skin. Because the 4B lead is soft and wax-heavy, the wet cloth only drives a faint grey shadow deeper into the pale wool.

Dropping the stained handkerchief onto the bench, I slip my bare right hand directly over the damp waistband of her bodice—pressing my palm flat and hard against her ribs, thumb over the front smudge, fingers spanning the three marks toward her spine, holding her waist still against my knee until my own heat goes all the way through the damp wool into her skin.

Above her collar, Lavinia's breath catches audibly.

“That's warm enough,” she whispers.

“It was still damp,” I say quietly, not taking my palm off her waist until her ribs rise twice against my hand.

Slowly, she pulls herself up from the floorboards and sits on the wooden bench beside me, leaving me kneeling on the boards by her feet. Her eyes in the mirror are wide and bright.

She reaches into her cardigan pocket, takes out a small tin of honey throat pastilles, and flicks the lid open with her thumb.

One amber square slips over the metal rim, strikes the pleat of her wrap skirt just above the knee, and stays lodged in the hollow of the wool.

Neither of us reaches for it with our fingers.

“You forgot lunch again, didn't you?” she says, her voice still slightly breathless.

“I had a biscuit.”

“That isn't lunch.” In the mirror behind her back, her reflected eyes stay locked on the crown of my head. “Go on, then.”

## 3. Beat 3 — The Insane Climax: `"It Was Hurting My Eyes"` & The Mirror Hold (`4A–4E`)

Below the edge of the bench, the pale satin ribbon around Lavinia's right ankle has come undone at the cross—tied in an unfamiliar square knot right over the tendon instead of folded flat behind the ankle bone where it belongs, one loose end trailing across the rosin.

Kneeling on the floorboards in front of her, I gather both satin ends to untie the square knot.

Just as my thumbs touch the cross, her heel slides half a step backward under the bench.

The satin slips free.

“Careful,” Lavinia says softly, watching the top of my head in the mirror. “You'll undo her knot.”

My hands stop dead above the floorboards.

In the wall-length glass behind the bench, Lavinia's eyes are fixed on my face—waiting to see what that sentence does to me.

“You tied this yourself,” I say.

“Did I?”

“She doesn't know how a ballet ribbon crosses the joint.”

“That's why it's a square knot,” Lavinia says smoothly, and as my fingers reach for her ankle a second time, she slides her heel another three inches back into the shadow beneath the bench—an awkward angle for her knee, baiting me to chase her foot under the seat. “Leave it. It holds well enough.”

### 4A — Never started: a question for a question

Both hands rest flat on my knees without chasing the shoe under the bench.

“Did you ask her to tie it, or did you leave the door unlatched so I'd think she did?”

“That isn't your business.”

“It will blister your tendon before Saturday.”

A beat of silence. Then her right foot slides back out from under the bench and stops an inch from my knee.

“Then tie it properly,” Lavinia says quietly. “Not too tight.”

The square knot comes undone; the satin crosses once, tucks flat behind the ankle bone where it belongs, and smooths down. When I sit back on the bench beside her, she leans her shoulder against mine and opens the paperback again.

“Read the rest of the page,” she says.

### 4B — Started, unfinished: the untied ends

Fingertips brush the leather heel under the bench, then open and draw back before closing around the arch.

“If you want a square knot on your tendon, keep it.”

In the mirror, Lavinia's chin tilts slightly.

“Stopping there?”

“The foot keeps moving.”

“You weren't so patient with my waist.”

Lavinia looks down at the crooked knot for three seconds, then bends, jerks the square knot undone with her own fingers, and reties it flat while her shoulder brushes my knee without telling me to leave.

### 4C — Won, not Perfect: the warm heel in the open palm

Instead of reaching into the shadow under the bench, my left palm stays open just above the floorboards where her shoe stood a moment ago.

“You always tie a square knot when you're in a hurry,” I say quietly. “Nobody else touched your shoe.”

“You sound very certain.”

“She ties left-over-right on her welfare packages. That knot is right-over-left.”

A short, reluctant breath leaves Lavinia's nose—half laugh, half sigh. Her heel slides forward out of the shadow and settles directly into my open palm.

The leather at the back of the shoe is warm.

“You're insufferable,” she mutters.

The heel stays cradled in my left palm while my right hand undoes the square knot and folds the satin flat behind the bone. When I lift my head, our eyes meet in the mirror glass behind her shoulder, and she draws me up onto the bench beside her.

### 4D — Lost: `"It Was Hurting My Eyes"`, the knee on the bench & the pulse hold (With Self-Talk Selector & Default)

Before her shoe can settle in the shadow under the bench, five fingers of my left hand lock around the arch and heel, draw her foot out from beneath the seat, and set her heel squarely on top of my own knee.

In the mirror, Lavinia's breath catches; her fingers tighten on the edge of the wooden bench.

“Let go.”

“No.”

Instead of easing the square knot open, my right hand catches the loose satin end and pulls it once, cleanly—stripping the square knot undone with a dry hiss of ribbon across her skin.

“She ties her welfare string like that so a parcel won't come undone in the post,” I say quietly, my thumb pressing into the warm hollow below her outer ankle bone so she cannot slide her foot a fraction of an inch back. “Leave a square knot over your tendon for ten minutes and it will bruise the skin before you even reach the barre.”

“It wasn't hurting me,” Lavinia whispers.

“It was hurting my eyes.”

My left palm stays wrapped around the warm tendon above her heel while my right hand crosses the pale satin twice—drawing the ribbon taut against her ankle, half a degree tighter than her usual rehearsal tension so she will feel the pressure of my fingers every time she points her foot—and tucks the flat fold behind the bone where no one else's hand can reach it without undoing mine.

Then, without taking my left hand off her ankle, I rise and plant my right knee directly on the wooden bench between her knees—leaning in until her shoulders meet the cool surface of the wall-length mirror and my right palm presses flat over the damp waistband of her bodice where the four pencil marks were.

Above my collar, Lavinia does not push my shoulder back. Instead, her right hand comes up and closes against the side of my neck—her fingers curling behind my ear, the pad of her thumb pressing hard into the pulse point just below my jaw.

In the glass behind her cheek, a small pale circle of condensation forms from her breath.

“You tied it too tight,” she whispers, her thumb pressing firmer into my throat.

“You'll remember who tied it,” I whisper against the corner of her mouth, my fingers tightening on her waist.

“And my ribs?” Her eyes blaze two inches from mine. “Are you going to keep your hand there until Friday so she sees it?”

“If I have to.”

For three full seconds neither of us moves in the mirror—my palm over the erased pencil marks at her waist, my other hand holding her ankle on the bench, her thumb pressed into the pulse at my throat.

“*Ești nebună,*” she breathes in Romanian, and then in English: “You're completely out of your mind.”
